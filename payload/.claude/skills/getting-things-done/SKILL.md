# Getting Things Done: The Art of Stress-Free Productivity

Implement a comprehensive personal productivity system to eliminate cognitive
load and maintain clarity across all projects and commitments, independent of
any specific tool, app, or platform.

## When to Activate

- You feel overwhelmed by the volume of tasks, emails, or commitments.
- Multiple projects are in flight and you can't track their status mentally.
- You want to reclaim mental energy currently spent remembering "stuff."
- You need a trustworthy system that prevents commitments from falling
  through gaps.
- You're struggling with prioritization because everything feels equally
  urgent.
- You keep re-deciding the same thing (what to do next) instead of deciding
  it once and trusting the system.

## Core Principle: A Trusted External System

Your mind is for having ideas, not holding them. Anything tracked in your
head consumes attention whether or not you're working on it — an open loop.
The goal is to capture every open loop in a system you trust completely, so
your mind stops rehearsing them and can focus on the present.

## The Five Stages of Workflow

1. **Capture** — Externalize everything with your attention into collection
   buckets (inbox, notebook, note app, voice memo, physical tray). Nothing is
   filtered, judged, or left in memory at this stage.
2. **Clarify** (a.k.a. Process) — Work each captured item to a decision: what
   is it, and does it require action? Define the very next physical action.
   Discard trivia, delegate what isn't yours, file reference-only items.
3. **Organize** — Put the results of clarification into the right category:
   projects list, next-action lists (by context), waiting-for list,
   someday/maybe list, calendar, reference storage.
4. **Reflect** (Review) — Step back regularly, especially weekly, to keep the
   system current, catch stalled items, and reconnect with the bigger
   picture.
5. **Engage** — Do the work. Choose, in the moment, which next action to take
   with confidence, because you trust the system holds everything else.

These are not sequential phases you finish once — they are five perspectives
you cycle through continuously, sometimes within seconds for one item.

## Core Concepts

**Two-Minute Rule:** If an action takes less than two minutes, do it
immediately instead of tracking it — the overhead of capturing, organizing,
and revisiting it exceeds the cost of just finishing it now. Exception: if
it isn't your job to do, delegate it instead.

**Next Actions:** The atomic unit of execution — a specific, physical,
visible action, phrased with a verb, that you could do right now without any
further decision.
- Good: *Call Priya re: Q3 budget number*, *Draft outline for onboarding doc*,
  *Buy replacement filter at hardware store*.
- Bad (not actions, they hide a decision): *Handle the budget issue*,
  *Website*, *Mom*, *Resolve the conflict*.

**Contexts:** Group next actions by the tool, location, person, or energy
level required to do them, not by priority or project. Typical contexts:
calls, at-computer, errands, home, office, agendas (per-person), waiting-for,
low-energy/read-review. When you're in a context, you scan only the list
that matches — everything on it is genuinely doable right now.

**Projects:** Any outcome that requires more than one action step to
complete counts as a project (e.g. "Organize team offsite," "Fix the
checkout bug," not just multi-year initiatives). A project without a defined
next action is a stalled project — the single most common failure mode.

**Horizons of Focus:** Priorities live at multiple altitudes, and healthy
review touches all of them, not just the ground floor:
- **Ground / Runway** — Next actions (the calls, errands, emails of today).
- **10,000 ft** — Current projects (outcomes needing multiple steps).
- **20,000 ft** — Areas of responsibility (roles you maintain: health,
  finances, a team, a product).
- **30,000 ft** — Goals for the next 1–2 years.
- **40,000 ft** — 3–5 year vision.
- **50,000 ft** — Life purpose and core values.
Work bottom-up in practice: get the ground level and 10,000 ft under control
first. Trying to plan from the top while the bottom is chaotic tends to
produce frustration, not clarity, because daily reality keeps interrupting
the higher-level thinking.

## Techniques

**Capture Phase**
- Maintain as few inboxes as you can manage consistently (one physical tray,
  one inbox app, one notebook) rather than the fewest theoretically possible.
- Capture every commitment, idea, worry, or task the instant it appears — no
  filtering, no judging "is this worth tracking" in the moment.
- Empty inboxes to zero on a regular cadence (daily or every couple of days);
  an inbox is a temporary holding pen, never permanent storage.

**Clarify/Process Phase**
- Process one item at a time, top to bottom, never skipping an item back
  into the inbox because it's hard — decide it now.
- Ask, in order:
  1. *What is this, exactly?*
  2. *Is it actionable?* If no → trash it, file it as reference, or park it
     on someday/maybe.
  3. *If yes, what's the very next physical action?*
  4. *Will it take less than two minutes?* → do it now.
  5. *Is this the best person to do it?* → delegate it, log it on
     waiting-for with a follow-up date.
  6. *Otherwise* → defer it: schedule on the calendar (if date-specific) or
     add to the matching context list.
- If a single action doesn't complete the outcome, it's a project: add it to
  the projects list and write its very next action separately.

**Organize Phase**
- **Projects list:** one line per project, reviewed as a whole, not
  micromanaged action-by-action from this list.
- **Next-action lists:** organized by context; reviewed only when you're in
  that context (e.g. review "calls" only when you have a phone free and time
  to make calls).
- **Waiting-for list:** what you delegated or are blocked on, who owns it,
  and the date you handed it off — so follow-ups happen by design, not
  surprise.
- **Calendar:** only for things with a hard date/time or day-specific
  actions — never a dumping ground for "someday" tasks.
- **Someday/Maybe list:** ideas and possible projects you're not committing
  to now but don't want to lose; revisit during weekly or less-frequent
  review.
- **Reference material:** filed by topic/project, retrievable in under a
  minute, entirely separate from action lists — reference is not actionable
  and shouldn't compete for attention with what you must do.

**Weekly Review**
A fixed, protected block (60–120 minutes), ideally the same time each week,
that keeps the whole system trustworthy:
1. Collect loose papers, notes, and digital scraps; empty every inbox.
2. Review the previous and upcoming calendar for actions and information to
   capture.
3. Walk the entire projects list; confirm each has a live next action.
4. Scan every next-action and waiting-for list for completeness and
   accuracy; check off what's done, delete what's stale.
5. Review someday/maybe; promote anything now actionable.
6. Glance up the horizons: any goals or areas of responsibility that need a
   new project or a course correction?
Skipping this step is the single fastest way the whole system decays — trust
in the lists comes directly from knowing they were reviewed recently.

**Engage Phase — choosing what to do now**
Given a trustworthy set of lists, pick the next action using, in order:
1. **Context** — what can you actually do right now, with what's available?
2. **Time available** — how long is this window?
3. **Energy available** — is this a high-focus or low-focus moment?
4. **Priority** — among what's left, what matters most?
Don't re-litigate whether an item belongs on the list while executing —
that decision was already made during clarify/organize. Mark items complete
only when the outcome is genuinely finished, not merely "worked on."

## Examples

- **Vague capture → clarified next action:** "Website" becomes "Draft three
  homepage headline options and send to design for feedback."
- **Delegation done right:** Ask a teammate to prepare a report → add
  "Waiting for: quarterly report from Sam, requested 2026-09-09" to the
  waiting-for list, with a follow-up date one week out.
- **Two-minute rule in action:** An email asking to confirm a meeting time →
  reply now, don't add "respond to email" to a list.
- **Project with a stalled next action:** "Launch internal wiki" sits on the
  projects list for a month with no action attached → during weekly review,
  add "Book 30-min kickoff with IT" as the next action to unstick it.
- **Context batching:** Instead of interrupting deep work to make a call,
  batch all "calls" context items into one block after lunch.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Incomplete capture:** Holding items in your head "because they're small"
  — small open loops still cost attention and create background anxiety.
- **Skipping clarification:** Storing vague items (*"handle project"*)
  instead of a discrete next physical action — the list becomes untrustworthy
  because nothing on it is truly doable.
- **Missing weekly review:** Without reflection, lists drift out of date,
  trust erodes, and you fall back to keeping track of things mentally again.
- **Priority-based lists instead of context-based:** Mixing all "urgent"
  items into one list wastes time and energy on constant context-switching.
- **Conflating projects and actions:** A project ("Redesign onboarding") is
  not itself doable — only its next action is. Writing projects onto an
  action list guarantees they'll stall.
- **Overthinking capture tools:** The best tool is the one you'll actually
  use consistently — sophistication is worthless without habit.
- **Delegating without a waiting-for tracker:** Forgotten delegations
  resurface as unpleasant surprises, or worse, never resurface at all.
- **Top-down planning while the bottom is chaotic:** Trying to set 3–5 year
  goals while daily next actions are undefined tends to produce frustration
  rather than clarity — stabilize the ground level and 10,000 ft first.
- **Using the calendar as a task dump:** Non-date-specific tasks crowd out
  the actual commitments and erode trust in the calendar as a hard-deadline
  source.
- **Treating "in progress" as done:** Marking an item complete before the
  outcome is genuinely achieved re-introduces the open loop later, at a
  worse time.

## Outcome

A trusted system eliminates background worry, recovers mental bandwidth, and
enables focus on meaningful work. Execution becomes fluid because decisions
about what things mean and what to do about them are made once, during
clarify and organize, freeing engage-time attention for doing the work
itself — at any horizon, from the next phone call to the multi-year vision.
